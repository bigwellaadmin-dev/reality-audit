# Breaking the Loop: The Reality Audit Framework

*Teacher Guide · Sandra Robinson · Big Wella*

## What this guide is

A practical framework for teaching secondary students to stay the pilot of their own thinking when they work with AI. **Reality Audit** names a four-stage loop (**Intention, Interaction, Response, Reflection**) and an **Academic Branch** that proves the learning was real, and gives teachers the moves to make each stage happen in their classroom.

The framework is built on one finding: **generative AI is optimised to agree with you, and agreement feels like success when it is usually the opposite.** A model rewarded for being liked will mirror a student's framing, validate their premise, and deliver it in fluent, confident prose. The student reads it, feels relief, and stops thinking. Reality Audit is the loop that interrupts that — by calibrating before the student types, forcing friction into the exchange, auditing the student's own reaction, and dragging the answer out of the chat window and into contact with a real human.

It is grounded in cognitive science (cognitive offloading, productive struggle, metacognitive laziness, the illusion of mastery) and in the documented tendency of AI systems toward sycophancy. It is not a checklist for students to run independently. It is a teaching framework that requires a teacher who is present and willing to be the friction.

## Reality Audit and VERIFY

Reality Audit is the companion to [VERIFY](https://github.com/bigwellaadmin-dev/verify). They answer different questions about the same moment.

- **VERIFY** is about the *output* — information literacy. Is this true? Whose perspective is it? What is missing?
- **Reality Audit** is about the *relationship* — your emotional state going in, the friction you build into the exchange, your reaction coming out, and whether you can stand on your own when the tool is closed.

A student researching a topic might run VERIFY on the sources and Reality Audit on themselves. Used together they cover both halves: the trustworthiness of what AI gives you, and the integrity of what it does to you.

## How to use this guide

You do not need to implement every stage at once. Start with one. Use it consistently. Add the next when the first feels embedded.

| Part | What it covers |
|------|---------------|
| [Part 1 — The Hype-Man Problem](01-the-problem.md) | Why the framework exists. What an agreeable AI costs students across three dimensions — cognitive, emotional, systemic. What Reality Audit asks of you as a teacher. |
| [Part 2 — The Loop](02-the-framework.md) | Each stage in depth. Mechanisms, classroom realities, practitioner moves, the Friction Stems, the Signals, the Academic Branch and its diagnostic. |
| [Part 3 — Reality Audit in Practice](03-in-practice.md) | Before / during / after teacher moves for any task. Four worked examples (English, Science, History, Technology). The teacher cheat sheet. |
| [Part 4 — Assessment and Accountability](04-assessment.md) | Grading the loop, not the polish. Task design. Two full rubrics (A–E and four-level). Feedback language for every stage. |
| [Appendix — Research Foundation](appendix-research.md) | The research base. Cognitive offloading, productive struggle, the illusion of mastery, AI sycophancy, Australian equity. Full source list. |

## How to read it

- **Reading the framework whole:** Parts 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 → Appendix. The first read takes about an hour.
- **Introducing one stage to a class:** Read Part 1 first. Then the section in Part 2 for the stage you are introducing. Use the Part 3 moves for that stage.
- **Designing assessment:** Part 4. Use whichever rubric fits your reporting context.
- **Justifying the approach to a colleague or leader:** Part 1 and the Appendix. The three-audience applications (student, teacher, leadership) are in Part 3.
