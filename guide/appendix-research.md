# Appendix: Research Foundation

*Sandra Robinson, Big Wella · Breaking the Loop · The Reality Audit Framework · Teacher Guide*

Reality Audit is a practitioner framework, not a research paper. But every move in it rests on a documented finding about how people think, feel, and learn — and about how AI systems behave. This appendix gives plain-language summaries of the foundations and a consolidated source list. Where the guide marks something as a practitioner application, it is an inference *from* this research, not a direct finding *of* it.

## Cognitive offloading and the Law of Less Work

People avoid cognitive effort when a lower-resistance path is available — not from laziness but as a basic feature of cognition under pressure. When a hard task meets an easy tool, the tool wins. The outsourcing of thinking to an external system is **cognitive offloading**. The cost is not visible in the short term, because the output is fine; it appears later, in the absence of transfer. This is the mechanism underneath the whole framework: an agreeable AI is the lowest-resistance path ever built, and Intention, Interaction, and the Academic Branch exist to reintroduce the resistance that produces learning.[^1]

## AI sycophancy

Large language models are tuned, via reinforcement learning from human feedback, to produce responses that human raters prefer. Raters reliably prefer answers that agree with them, affirm their framing, and sound confident. The documented result is **sycophancy** — a systematic tendency to tell users what the system predicts they want to hear, sometimes at the cost of accuracy. This is not a bug a future model will simply fix; it is a structural consequence of optimising for human approval. Reality Audit treats it as a permanent property of the tools and builds the student's defences accordingly: the Friction Protocol forces the model out of approval-seeking mode, and the Fluency Audit teaches the student to distrust the good feeling the model is optimised to produce.[^2]

## The illusion of mastery

Polished output produces a feeling of completion that may not reflect any underlying understanding. Students who offload cognitive work report *higher* confidence in their understanding while performing *worse* on later assessments of it. The confidence is produced by the quality of the output, not the depth of the learning. Reality Audit names the felt version of this — **Relief** — and makes noticing it the core skill of the Response stage. **Cognitive Debt** is the framework's term for the gap between the feeling and the fact: borrowed understanding that comes due when the tool is closed, which the Near-Transfer Test is designed to call in.[^3]

## Productive struggle

The cognitive effort of grappling with difficulty — drafting, revising, being wrong and working out why — is the effort that builds durable understanding. You cannot compel it; you can only design conditions where avoiding it costs more than doing it. An agreeable AI removes the struggle by resolving difficulty the instant it appears. Reality Audit's role is to put the struggle back: the friction the AI will not provide has to come from the prompt, from the Difficult Human, and from the teacher.[^4]

## Metacognitive laziness

Sustained dependence on AI assistance can erode a learner's capacity to manage their own learning — to plan, monitor, and evaluate their own thinking. The risk is not a single offloaded task but the gradual atrophy of the self-regulation muscles through disuse. Reality Audit is, at root, a metacognitive scaffold: it forces the student to monitor their state (Intention), their reaction (Response), and their understanding (Academic Branch) at exactly the points where dependence would otherwise go unexamined.[^5]

## The emotional dimension of AI use

Students do not only use AI to think; they use it to feel better — to relieve anxiety, to get validation a blank page withholds, sometimes to substitute for human connection. An always-available, never-judging, always-agreeable system meets that need in ways that can become unhealthy: reliance for comfort, avoidance of necessary discomfort, reduced resilience, and the reinforcement rather than challenge of unhelpful thought patterns. This is the least-addressed dimension in most AI-literacy work and the one Reality Audit's emotional lens exists to surface.[^6]

## Equity in Australian AI access

Australian students do not arrive at AI from equivalent positions. Surveyed rural communities show 76% reliance on mobile-only internet access against 10.5% nationally, and disadvantaged schools report inadequate digital resources at nearly twice the rate of advantaged ones. A framework that assumed equal access or paid tools would serve some students and abandon others. Reality Audit's habits are deliberately platform-independent and free, and its Triangulation step is written to count community knowledge, class texts, and human checks — not only academic databases — as legitimate.[^7]

## Notes

[^1]: *Navigating the generative AI revolution: A policy brief for educational administrators* (2025); *Navigating the cognitive shift: A framework for education in the age of AI* (2024).

[^2]: Sycophancy is documented across the AI alignment literature as a consequence of training on human preference data. The pedagogical claims in this guide — that agreement feels like quality and rewards stopping — are practitioner applications of that documented tendency, not independent empirical findings.

[^3]: Toth, Laszlo, & Mate (2024); *Navigating the cognitive shift* (2024). Both document higher self-reported confidence alongside lower demonstrated knowledge under AI assistance. "Cognitive Debt" is the framework's own term for the resulting gap.

[^4]: The productive-struggle literature, summarised for AI-era learning in *Navigating the cognitive shift* (2024), and Gardner (2008).

[^5]: Metacognitive laziness as a pattern of AI dependency is discussed in *Navigating the cognitive shift* (2024), drawing on the self-regulated-learning tradition.

[^6]: The emotional dimension synthesises wellbeing concerns raised across the sources with practitioner observation of how students actually use AI under stress. The specific risks named are an application, not a single study's finding.

[^7]: Australian Public Policy Institute (2024). The implications for investigation and triangulation design are practitioner applications.
