# Part 3: Reality Audit in Practice

*Sandra Robinson, Big Wella · Breaking the Loop · The Reality Audit Framework · Teacher Guide*

Part 2 explained each stage. Part 3 is about running the loop in a real classroom: how to introduce it, the moves across a full task, worked examples by subject, the teacher cheat sheet, and how the framework scales from a single student to a whole-school policy.

## Introducing the loop

Do not introduce all four stages at once. Start with the two that produce the most immediate, visible behaviour change: **Intention** and **Response**.

- **Week 1 — Intention + Response.** Students declare RAG before any AI task and name Relief or Curiosity after reading the output. Two habits, both concrete, both fast. Use a low-stakes task to practise.
- **Week 2 — add Interaction.** Introduce the Friction Stems. The shift from "ask the AI" to "argue with the AI" is the heart of the framework; give it its own week.
- **Week 3 — add Reflection and the Academic Branch.** Bring in the Difficult Human and the Near-Transfer Test. Show the full loop as a loop, with the diagram, on an authentic task.
- **Ongoing.** Make the loop visible — a poster, the stems on a card, the RAG colours on the wall. Debrief significant AI interactions in Reality Audit language until the language is the students' own.

## The moves across a full task

The same task, seen through the four stages, gives you a before / during / after rhythm.

### Before students open the tool

- Set the RAG routine: colour plus one honest sentence.
- Put the Friction Stems where students can see them.
- Name the Difficult Human they will consult and the Near-Transfer check that is coming. Knowing the closed-tool test is coming changes how students work the whole way through.

### While students work

- **Calibration check:** *"What colour are you, and why are you asking it that?"*
- **Friction check:** *"Show me where you asked it to disagree with you."*
- **Reaction check:** *"Relief or curiosity — which one are you feeling right now?"* (Ask for the feeling before the analysis.)
- **Circuit-break:** when a student has been agreeing with the bot too long, send them to a human.

### After the work

- Collect the **Interaction Log** as a first-class artefact, not an afterthought.
- Run a **Near-Transfer Test** on a related problem, tool closed.
- Ask for the **Triangulation trace**: what outside source or human confirmed the key claim.

> **The teacher-presence principle**
>
> None of these moves work if the teacher checks the output and not the process. An agreeable AI produces output that looks finished at every stage. The only way to know whether thinking happened is to interrupt the loop and look — at the colour, the friction, the reaction, the cold reproduction. A teacher who skips the check because the work looks good has missed the entire point of Reality Audit.

## Worked examples

Four examples, one per subject, each showing where the loop bites for that kind of task. They are patterns, not scripts.

### English — Year 10, persuasive essay

A student writing on social media and adolescent mental health pastes their thesis and asks the AI what it thinks. The AI calls it "a compelling and nuanced argument." **Relief.** The student feels finished.

- **Intention:** They were Amber — they had a position but foggy reasoning — and they asked the AI to validate, which is the Green move. Miscalibrated.
- **Interaction:** The fix is a Friction Stem: *"Argue against me. Prove my perspective is one-sided."* The AI surfaces the obvious counter — that correlation between social media use and poor mental health is not causation — which the student had ignored.
- **Response:** The discomfort the student now feels is Curiosity. That is the signal the work has started.
- **Reflection:** They take the sharpened argument to a skeptical friend who actually uses the platforms in question, and the lived-experience pushback reshapes a paragraph.
- **Academic Branch:** Near-Transfer — can they argue the *opposite* thesis cold? If yes, they own the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

### Science — Year 9, environmental issue report

A student researching a local water-quality issue asks the AI for "the main causes." It returns a confident, generic, northern-hemisphere-flavoured list.

- **Systemic lens fires:** *"This assumes a perspective that may not be mine."* The list does not mention the specific local catchment or the agricultural runoff that dominates it.
- **Interaction:** *"What is the most uncomfortable truth about my current approach?"* surfaces that the student has been reasoning about water in general, not their water.
- **Reflection / Triangulation:** they confirm against the local council's monitoring data — a source the AI could not have. The Triangulation Protocol does real work here.
- **Academic Branch:** Document the Process — the log shows the pivot from generic to local, which is the learning.

### History — Year 8, causes of WWI

A student asks for "the causes of World War I" and gets a fluent, authoritative summary. It feels complete.

- **Response:** Relief — it sounds like a textbook, so it must be right. The audit question: *whose history is in this answer?*
- **Systemic lens:** the summary centres the European great-power narrative and omits the colonial dimension entirely. Nothing on the surface flagged the omission.
- **Interaction:** *"Find three flaws in this account"* makes the AI name its own gaps.
- **Reflection:** the student takes the revised account to the teacher, who pushes on one claim. The defence either holds or sends them back into the loop.

### Technology / Design — Year 9, community garden app

A student uses an image generator and a chatbot to design an app concept and feels a strong pull to accept the polished first output.

- **Emotional lens:** *"I feel relieved — this looks professional."* The relief is doing the deciding. The Intention check: were they exploring, or avoiding the harder design thinking?
- **Interaction:** iterative prompting *as* the craft — each round asks the AI to critique the last, not to bless it.
- **Academic Branch:** Near-Transfer — can the student justify each design decision without the tool, to a peer? If the rationale only exists in the chat, the design is borrowed.

## The teacher cheat sheet

Phrases for talking to a student about their AI use, in the moment. (Reproduced from the framework.)

- *"I see the AI gave you a 10/10. Now, show me the prompt where you asked it to find your mistakes."*
- *"You've been in a loop with this bot for an hour. Go find someone who disagrees with you."*
- *"This sounds like 'Hype Man' writing. It's too polite. Where is YOUR voice in this?"*
- *"You've marked yourself as Green, so why are you asking the AI for an introduction? Ask it to find a flaw in your thesis instead."*
- *"You seem to be in the Relief state. Close the laptop and tell me in your own words what you just learned."*
- *"I see a great answer. Show me the Triangulation Protocol — where did the textbook confirm this?"*

## The framework at three scales

Reality Audit is the same loop whether you are guiding one student, planning a unit, or writing a policy. The lens just widens.

### For students — building AI literacy

The student-facing payoff is three habits: **reflective questioning** (Am I using AI to avoid necessary struggle?), **prompt craft** (moving from "do this for me" to a deliberate, friction-rich exchange), and **balanced reliance** (judgement about which tasks need their own cognitive effort). The emotional layer is the differentiator — students learn to notice when they are turning to AI out of anxiety rather than curiosity, and to act on it.

### For teachers — guiding integration

Reality Audit gives you three things: an **instructional-design lens** (which objectives need independent work; how to make AI use visible rather than hidden), a **classroom-dialogue language** (the signals, the stems, the cheat sheet), and a **risk-assessment habit** (spotting assignments vulnerable to frictionless shortcuts and students showing dependence). Crucially, it offers an alternative to the detect-and-punish model of integrity: design assessments that make AI use transparent and reflective, and assess the decision-making, not just the artefact.

### For leadership — a living AI policy

For school leaders, Reality Audit anchors a **living policy** — one that sets stable expectations while accepting that specific tools will keep changing. It avoids the two failure patterns of technology policy: unenforceable bans and vague guidelines that direct no one. Because Reality Audit governs *process and principle* rather than specific tools, a policy built on it does not need rewriting every time a new model launches. It gives a school a shared language across subjects and year levels, balances structure with flexibility, and — through the three lenses — keeps wellbeing and equity in the policy conversation alongside academic integrity.

> **The principle that scales**
>
> The most effective AI policy is not the one that draws the cleanest line between permitted and prohibited. It is the one that builds the capacity for ethical judgement, so that students make good decisions in situations no policy anticipated. Reality Audit is built to teach that judgement, which is why it works at every scale.
