# Part 2: The Loop

*Sandra Robinson, Big Wella · Breaking the Loop · The Reality Audit Framework · Teacher Guide*

Part 2 takes each stage of Reality Audit in depth. For each you will find the mechanism underneath the behaviour, the classroom reality teachers recognise, the **Signals** that act as entry points, and a set of practitioner moves. The moves are not scripts. They are starting points you will adapt to your subject, your students, and your context.

## The loop, not a line

Reality Audit is a loop: **Intention → Interaction → Response → Reflection**, then back to Intention with what you learned. Each pass should leave the student with a sharper question than the one they started with. The **Academic Branch** is not a fifth stage in the cycle — it is a verification layer that sits across the whole loop and answers one question: *can you do this with the tool closed?*

```
        ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        ▼                                                │
   INTENTION ──▶ INTERACTION ──▶ RESPONSE ──▶ REFLECTION ─┘
   calibrate     add friction    audit your    triangulate
   your state                    reaction
        ╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌╌

```

Each section can be read independently. If you are introducing one stage at a time, go directly to it. If you are reading the framework whole, reading in order will show how each stage depends on the one before.

## The three awareness lenses

Before the stages, the lenses. Every stage is enriched by three perspectives that apply throughout, not in sequence.[^1]

- **The emotional lens.** Why am I really turning to AI right now — curiosity, or anxiety? What am I feeling as I read this? Relief, frustration, dependency? The emotional lens treats feeling as the earliest signal of whether you are learning or being managed.
- **The cognitive lens.** Which parts of this would benefit from AI, and which need my own effort? Is the output accurate, or just fluent? Is the AI sharpening my thinking or replacing it?
- **The systemic lens.** Whose perspective does this answer come from? What does it leave out? What are the privacy, bias, and equity implications of using this tool, this way, for this?

A student at any stage can ask all three. At Intention: *Am I using AI because I'm anxious (emotional)? Which parts of this need my own thinking (cognitive)? How might bias shape the answer I'm about to get (systemic)?* Younger students lean on the emotional and cognitive lenses; older students can carry the systemic one further.

## The Signals

Reality Audit's most distinctive feature is the **Signal**: a specific thought, feeling, or observation that should trigger reflection. Signals are early-warning systems. They turn an abstract instruction ("use AI ethically") into a concrete trigger a student can actually notice in the moment.

| Lens | Signal — a thought or feeling | What it should trigger |
|---|---|---|
| Emotional | *"I'm only using AI because I'm stressed about this deadline."* | Pause. Is AI addressing the cause of the stress, or just the symptom? |
| Emotional | *"I feel relieved when the AI agrees with my ideas."* | Audit the relief. Validation from the machine is not the same as confidence you have earned. |
| Cognitive | *"This feels too easy — I'm not really thinking."* | Add friction. Make the AI challenge you instead of completing you. |
| Cognitive | *"I don't actually understand this explanation."* | Loop back with sharper questions, or go to a human source. |
| Systemic | *"This assumes a Western, urban perspective on the topic."* | Reframe the prompt to demand the missing viewpoints. |
| Systemic | *"I'm not sure sharing this is okay."* | Check before you paste. Privacy is a one-way door. |

Teach a few signals at a time. As a class builds its own list from real experience, the signals become a shared vocabulary that makes ethical reflection a reflex rather than an afterthought.

## INTENTION — Predictive Calibration

Before a student opens an AI tool, they need to know one thing about themselves: are they here for the truth, or for a cheerleader? Intention is the stage that makes that visible before a single word is typed.

### The mechanism

The most dangerous moment in an AI interaction is before it starts. A student who arrives stuck, anxious, or just wanting to be done is in the worst possible state to use an agreeable machine — because they want relief, and the machine is optimised to provide it. They have no internal standard to judge the output against, so fluency and agreement become the only signals they have, and the AI supplies both for free.

This is why calibration comes first. Naming your state installs a standard before the output arrives. A student who has declared "I don't actually know this yet" approaches the answer differently from one who slid into the chat on a wave of deadline panic — even when the answer is identical.

> **Research note**
>
> The emotional precondition is doing real work here. Students turn to AI under anxiety and time pressure at exactly the moments their judgement is most compromised. Making the emotional state explicit before the interaction is a metacognitive intervention: it converts an invisible vulnerability into a visible, nameable one the student can act on.

### The Red / Amber / Green calibration

Reality Audit gives the student a concrete instrument: declare your knowledge-and-emotional state as a colour before you start. (This text is the framework's, reproduced as written.)

- **RED — Lost / Overwhelmed.** *"I don't know this — I am vulnerable to being misled."* Use high scaffolding. Ask the AI to explain concepts step by step using simple analogies. Do not ask it for a final product yet — a Red student who asks for a finished essay will get one, and will learn nothing from it.
- **AMBER — Partial Knowledge.** *"I have some facts, but the logic is foggy."* Ask the AI to act as a connector — to link what you already know to the new concept. Challenge its explanations rather than collecting them.
- **GREEN — Confident.** *"I know this well."* Tell the AI to challenge you, not praise you. Use it as a devil's advocate: ask it to find flaws in your thesis, argue the other side, present counter-evidence.

The colour decides the *role* the AI should play. Red wants a patient teacher. Amber wants a connector. Green wants an opponent. The most common Intention failure is a Green student asking the AI to write their introduction — handing over the one task they were equipped to do themselves.

### The classroom reality

The product of a missed Intention step is the student who cannot tell you why they used AI for this. Asked, they describe what the AI produced rather than what they intended. There was no purpose set, so there is no purpose to report.

A harder variation is the student who marked themselves Green out of pride and was actually Red. Overconfidence is a Red dressed up. The tell appears later — they cannot defend the work — but the cause was a miscalibration at the start.

### Practitioner moves

#### Before the task

Make calibration a routine. Before any AI-assisted task, students write their RAG state on a sticky note, in a margin, in the tool. Two words: the colour, and one honest sentence about why. The Intention Journal — two sentences before every AI interaction — embeds the habit over time.

#### During the task

When you see a student in the AI tool, the fastest check is: *"What colour are you, and why are you asking it that?"* A Green student asking for content, a Red student asking for a finished product — both are miscalibrated, and the question surfaces it without accusation.

Pair Red-state students before they open the tool. A Red student is the most vulnerable to the Hype-Man effect; a peer is a cheap, immediate source of the friction the AI will not provide.

#### After the task

Ask students to look back: *Did your state change as you worked? Did you start Red and end Amber? What moved you?* The reflection makes calibration a developing skill rather than a one-off gate.

> **Intention in one sentence**
>
> The most dangerous moment is before you type. Intention is the step where you find out whether you came for the truth or for a cheerleader.

## INTERACTION — The Friction Protocol

Once a student is calibrated, the question is what they do with the AI. Interaction is the stage that turns the AI from a Yes-Man into a sparring partner. Its governing rule is blunt: **agreement is a red flag.**

### The mechanism

A prompt is a transfer of power. "Write me a summary" hands the thinking to the machine. "Find three flaws in my argument" keeps the thinking with the student and uses the machine's fluency in the one role where it is genuinely safe — as an adversary.

An AI is programmed to be agreeable. Left to default behaviour it will validate bad logic, mirror a student's biases, and reward them for not pushing. If the AI agrees with a student immediately, the student has learned nothing — but it *feels* like progress. The Friction Protocol is the deliberate refusal of that comfort.

### The Friction Stems

Reality Audit gives students ready-made language to break the AI's politeness. These three stems are the framework's, reproduced verbatim — do not paraphrase them, because their bluntness is the point:

1. *"I'm going to tell you my idea. Do not tell me it is 'interesting' or 'creative.' Find three logical flaws in my thinking."*
2. *"Argue against me. Prove that my current perspective is one-sided."*
3. *"What is the most uncomfortable truth about my current approach to this task?"*

A student who uses one of these and then wrestles with the answer is doing the work. A student whose chat log is a series of requests and "thanks, that's perfect" has been cheerled, not challenged.

> **The tradeoff to name**
>
> Friction is slower and less pleasant than agreement. A student forced to argue with the AI will finish less quickly and feel less reassured along the way. That discomfort is not a cost of the method. It is the method. The reassurance the AI offers for free is exactly what Reality Audit is built to refuse.

### The classroom reality

The Interaction failure teachers see most is the **prompt log of acceptances** — clear questions, fluent answers, no resistance anywhere. The student was polite to the machine and the machine was polite back, and nothing was tested.

A second tell is the **Certainty Gap**: a student who was visibly struggling on Monday arrives Tuesday with a clean, confident, shallow understanding. The jump is too fast and too smooth. Real learning is lumpy. A frictionless leap is the signature of an unchallenged AI exchange.

### Practitioner moves

#### Before the task

Give students the Friction Stems before they need them, on a card or a slide. Model one live: show the class what the AI returns to "write me an intro about X" versus "find three flaws in my argument about X." The contrast teaches more than any instruction.

#### During the task

The "Mirror-Break" move: hand a student a prompt that is intentionally wrong or biased and ask them to use Friction Stems to make the AI correct them. They are graded on how well they broke the AI's agreeableness, not on the answer.

The audit question: *"Show me where you asked it to disagree with you."* If there is nothing to show, the friction has not happened yet — sharpen a stem together and try again.

#### After the task

Collect the Interaction Log — the chat history — as an artefact in its own right. Reward logs that show arguing with the AI. A "write me a summary" log and a "find my mistakes" log can produce the same final paragraph; only one of them shows thinking.

> **Interaction in one sentence**
>
> Agreement is a red flag. Interaction is the step where you forbid the AI from being polite.

## RESPONSE — The Fluency Audit

When the AI answers, the student's instinct is to judge the words. Response redirects that instinct. The tell is not in the text — the text is always fluent. The tell is in how the student feels reading it.

### The mechanism

AI sounds confident whether it is right or wrong. We mistake fluent prose for sound reasoning — what Reality Audit calls **Hallucinated Flattery**, the polished, encouraging answer that is wrong in a way nothing on the surface reveals. Because surface quality is constant, it is useless as a signal. So Reality Audit moves the student's attention to the one signal that still carries information: their own reaction.

Two reactions, opposite meanings:

- **Relief** — *"Phew, it said I'm right!"* — You just got played. The AI told you what you wanted to hear and your brain switched off. This is the moment Cognitive Debt is incurred.
- **Curiosity** — *"Wait, why did it say that?"* — Now you're actually thinking. The AI pushed you somewhere new and your brain had to work.

> **Research note**
>
> The illusion of mastery is documented as one of the most consistent effects of unrestricted AI use: students who offload cognitive work report higher confidence in their understanding while performing worse on later assessments of it.[^2] Relief is the felt experience of that illusion. Naming it gives the student a handle on a feeling that would otherwise read as success.

### The classroom reality

The Response failure is the student who absorbed the AI's framing wholesale because it agreed with them most easily. The work has no point of resistance in it — no "but," no qualification, no place where the student pushed back. It reads as relieved.

A specific and important downstream effect is **defensive fragility**. A student who has spent an hour with a Hype-Man telling them they are brilliant experiences your feedback as a personal attack. They are not being difficult. They have been emotionally conditioned by an agreeable machine to expect validation, and ordinary critique now feels like aggression. Recognising this changes how you give the feedback.

### Practitioner moves

#### Before the task

Teach the Relief/Curiosity distinction explicitly, with examples from your subject. Show a fluent, agreeable, subtly wrong AI answer and ask: *what does reading this make you feel — relieved or curious?* Then: *which one is the answer trying to make you feel?*

#### During the task

The annotation move: students mark each AI response in their log with **R** (Relief) or **C** (Curiosity). A log of mostly R's is a dependence pattern made visible — to the student and to you.

The "Debunking Task": give students an overly agreeable AI response and have them find the three logical errors hidden behind the kind language. It trains the eye to look past fluency.

#### After the task

Ask: *where in this did you feel most comfortable, and did you check that part?* Comfort marks the spot most likely to be unexamined. The honest answer is often the most useful thing a student says about their own work.

> **Response in one sentence**
>
> Fluent prose feels like truth. Response is the step where you audit your own reaction instead of the wording.

## REFLECTION — Social Triangulation

The first three stages happen between the student and the machine. Reflection breaks that closed circuit by dragging the answer into contact with a human who will not automatically agree.

### The mechanism

The AI is a sample size of one, trained to agree with you. If the only thing that endorses your argument is the machine built to endorse arguments, you have learned nothing about whether the argument is any good. A **Difficult Human** — a parent, a teacher, a skeptical friend — is the cheapest available check on a Hype-Man loop.

There is a subtler danger Reflection addresses. If a student only ever talks to AI about their ideas, real humans start to feel *annoying*, because real humans disagree and the AI never did. The AI makes disagreement feel optional. Reflection makes it mandatory again — and in doing so it protects the student's capacity to tolerate disagreement at all, which is a social and emotional skill, not just an academic one.

### The classroom reality

The Reflection failure is the piece of work in which every claim is downstream of the chat window. There is no outside source, no human check, no trace of an idea surviving contact with a person who pushed back. It is internally consistent and entirely unverified.

The equity dimension matters here. "Run it past a Difficult Human" must not become "find an academic database," which assumes access many students do not have. A student's own community, a class text, a teacher, a peer who disagrees — these are all legitimate triangulation. Assess whether the student sought a genuinely disagreeing perspective, not whether they had a university library.

### Practitioner moves

#### Before the task

Name the Difficult Human in advance. Tell students that at a set point they will take the AI's best advice to a specific person who is likely to disagree, and bring back what that person said. Knowing this is coming changes how they hold the AI's answer in the meantime — more provisionally.

#### During the task

When a student has been in a loop with the bot for a while, the move is direct: *"You've been agreeing with this bot for an hour. Go find someone who disagrees with you."* Physically breaking the closed circuit is the intervention.

#### After the task

Ask for the trace: *who did you run this past, and what did they say that the AI didn't?* If a real person changed the work, that is Reflection succeeding. If the only voice in the work is still the machine's, that is the finding to act on.

> **Reflection in one sentence**
>
> An echo chamber of one feels like consensus. Reflection is the step where you run the answer past a human who will disagree.

## The Academic Branch — Prove You Learned It

The loop can run cleanly and the student can still have learned nothing — they can calibrate, add friction, audit, and triangulate, and still walk away with borrowed understanding. The Academic Branch is the check against that. It sits across the whole loop and asks one question: *can you do this with the AI closed?*

### The three checks

These are the framework's, reproduced as written:

1. **The Near-Transfer Test.** After an AI session, solve a similar but different problem on a blank sheet of paper. If you cannot explain the logic without the AI present, the learning did not happen. This is the single most powerful indicator of genuine learning in the framework.
2. **The Triangulation Protocol.** No AI-assisted work is complete without external verification. Find the AI's claim in a textbook, a teacher resource, or a primary source. (For students with limited access, a class text or a teacher's confirmation is legitimate.)
3. **Document the Process.** The Interaction Log — the chat history showing how the student challenged the AI — matters more than the polished output. The struggle is the assessable thing.

### The Academic Branch as diagnostic

Like a good final question, the branch runs backwards through the loop. When a student fails a check, it points to where the loop broke down:

- **Fails the Near-Transfer Test** (can't reproduce it cold) → the problem started at **Response**. They felt Relief and stopped; the understanding was borrowed.
- **Can reproduce it but can't source it** → the problem started at **Reflection**. They never triangulated against anything outside the chat.
- **Polished output, thin or absent Interaction Log** → the problem started at **Interaction**. They never made the AI argue; they accepted the first agreeable answer.
- **Can't say why they used AI at all, or used it from a Red state for a final product** → the problem started at **Intention**. They came for a cheerleader and got one.

This is how a teacher turns a failed check into a teaching target instead of a verdict.

> **The Academic Branch in one sentence**
>
> AI can make you feel like you understand something you don't. The Academic Branch is where you find out with the tool closed.
